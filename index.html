Let's Break The Ice...

I am a researcher passionate about working at the intersection of advanced engineering and modern medicine. My academic training and research expertise span multiple disciplines, including chemical engineering and materials science, tissue engineering and regenerative medicine, and computational biology. I leverage my experience in wet-lab techniques and computational methods to address clinically relevant problems while collaborating closely with engineers, clinicians, histo-pathologists, and veterinarians. Currently, as a postdoctoral fellow in the Department of Oncology at Johns Hopkins School of Medicine, I am working toward developing a mechanistic understanding of prostate cancer progression to guide biomarker-based therapeutic strategies using advanced multi-omics platforms.

My foray into the world of research began as an accidental chemical engineer/materials scientist. I began experimenting with various synthesis chemistries and protocols to produce carbon- or silica-based porous materials, which are indispensable to the field of catalysis and energy storage. Along the way, I was introduced to the world of gels, including xerogels, aerogels, and particularly hydrogels – a soft, squishy, jelly-like material whose mechanical property can be tailored to mimic that of the extracellular matrix found in living tissue. This makes hydrogel a popular biomaterial to grow and culture living cells outside the body, while their tunable viscoelastic property allows them to be fabricated into intricate shapes and geometries by the process of 3D-printing. The idea of growing biological tissues and organs from scratch, using biomaterials as building blocks, piqued my curiosity. This curiosity compelled me to explore the field further, eventually leading me to pursue a PhD in tissue engineering and regenerative medicine.

The human face provides a unique identity to every individual and accommodates several vital tissues and sensory organs. Consequently, any major injury to the face compromises normal physiology, self-esteem and can be life threatening. As medieval it may sound, the current gold standard treatment strategy to treat large facial bone injuries in patients is to take bone from one part of the body and transplant it at the injury site. This treatment has several limitations and leads to poor clinical outcomes. The lack of a robust treatment strategy motivated me to dedicate my doctoral research to developing a bone tissue engineering strategy to address the limitations of the existing treatment and advance the field with regards to management of midfacial bone injuries. Through two major pre-clinical studies and a year-long follow-up, we demonstrated the feasibility and therapeutic efficacy of our treatment strategy. (more in detail here)

Through my PhD journey, I realized that a deep mechanistic understanding of tissue regeneration in response to the treatment is very essential from a regulatory standpoint to achieve clinical translation. However, limited availability of tissue samples, coupled with the destructive nature of most characterization techniques make it extremely challenging to perform extensive molecular profiling of the regenerative environment. Although, polymerase chain reaction (qPCR), immunostaining, gel-electrophoresis experiments are very informative and have been the workhorse for modern medicine, they only tell a part of the story. Thus, I decided to explore the field of spatial biology and leverage the high-throughput and multiplexing capabilities of state-of-the-art multi-omics platforms to understand the underlying complex biology of disease, with the ultimate goal to augment and enhance clinical diagnosis and decision-making.

One such clinically relevant scenario is that of kidney transplantation wherein patients with kidney failure depend on kidney allografts obtained from living or deceased donors for treatment. However, the donor and recepients are usually loacated at geographically distant sites. As such, kidney allografts are usually shipped on ice between the sites to maintain organ viability prior to transplantation. Prolonged duration on ice is known to induce cold ischemia injury within the kidney allografts and is associated with poor clinical outcomes. Unfortunately, there is a lack of mechanistic understanding of the pathophysiology of cold ischemia kidney injury and how it affects kidney allograft function and survival after transplantation. To bridge this knowledge gap, we performed full transcriptome profiling of murine kidneys subjected to extended durations of cold ischemia injury using 10x Visium spatial transcriptomics platform. Through our molecular characterization, we identified compartment-specific transcriptional changes which may have clinical implications in the poor prognosis associated with kidney allografts with significant cold ischemia injury. At this juncture of my research journey, I am applying my learnings and expertise in a clinical research setting. I feel that clinical research presents unique challenges which are often less pronounced in well-controlled pre-clinical animal experiments. For example, small sample sizes and heterogeneity of patient cohort in clinical datasets and limited tissue availability to perform multiple assays can constrain experimental analyses and the ability to draw meaningful conclusions. This is where I believe the recent technological advances in imaging, molecular probing and computational techniques can make a signifcant difference in clincal diagnosis and management. Currently, I am developing multi-omics based workflows to enable mechanistic understanding of prostate cancer in human patients, with the goal of translating molecular insights into clinically relevant biomarker-based therapeutic strategies.
